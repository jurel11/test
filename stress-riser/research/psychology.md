# Psychology & visual perception of clicking - research notes (Stress Riser)
Date: 2026-09-30. Grades: [A] official/peer-reviewed/large dataset, [B] first-hand creator/company data, [C] opinion/anecdote.
Abstracts were retrieved programmatically (Europe PMC / PubMed / OpenAlex / Crossref APIs, or PDF text) unless flagged "from memory".

## 1. CURIOSITY

### 1.1 Information-gap theory
- Loewenstein, G. (1994). The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation. Psychological Bulletin 116(1), 75-98. DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75. [A]
  Verified only via citing papers (Le Quere & Matias 2025 quotes Golman & Loewenstein's definition of an information gap as "an awareness of the absence of potentially useful or interesting information"). Core claims (from secondary sources, not the scanned PDF which I could not OCR): curiosity = perceived gap between what one knows and wants to know; a small "priming dose" of information increases curiosity; curiosity is stronger when the gap is salient AND resolvable. NOTE: one web-search summary claimed "larger gaps lead to low curiosity" - this is a garbled paraphrase; the theory says gap size RELATIVE TO WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW matters. Treat with care.
- Golman & Loewenstein (2018). Information gaps: A theory of preferences regarding the presence and absence of information. Decision 5(3), 143-164. DOI 10.1037/dec0000068. [A] (abstract: "curiosity - the desire to fill information gaps"; "attention devoted" to a gap matters)
- Kang, Hsu, Krajbich, Loewenstein, McClure, Wang, Camerer (2009). The wick in the candle of learning. Psychological Science 20(8), 963-973. DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02402.x. PMID 19619181. [A]
  Verified abstract: curiosity when reading trivia questions correlated with caudate activity (anticipated reward); people paid tokens/waiting time to learn answers when more curious; higher curiosity -> better recall of surprising answers 1-2 weeks later.
  Inverted-U (verified via Le Quere & Matias 2025, Sci Rep, PDF text): "people were most curious about the answer to a trivia question when their confidence in their answer was middling, between 0.45 and 0.55 on a scale from 0 to 1".
- Ten, Oudeyer, Sakaki, Murayama (2025). "The Curious U: Integrating Theories Linking Knowledge and Curiosity" Open Mind (PMC12618012). [A - perspective/review] Synthesises 7 theories of the inverted-U between knowledge and curiosity; curiosity peaks at INTERMEDIATE knowledge.
- Kidd, Piantadosi & Aslin (2012). The Goldilocks effect. PLoS ONE 7(5): e36399. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0036399. [A] Infants (7-8 mo) look away from events that are too predictable or too surprising; attention peaks at intermediate surprisingness. Infants -> weak direct transfer to adults viewing thumbnails; supports the principle "not too simple, not too complex".
- Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath (2014). Neuron 84(2): 486-496. DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2014.08.060. [A] High curiosity -> midbrain/nucleus accumbens activity, better memory of the target AND incidental material. (Relevance: curiosity raised by a thumbnail should improve retention of the story.)
- Kidd & Hayden (2015). The psychology and neuroscience of curiosity. Neuron 88(3): 449-460. DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.010. [A review]
- Frede, Bardach, Strittmatter, Richter, Mueckstein & Spitzer (2026). "Knowledge Gap Illustrations Spark Curiosity". Journal of Cognition (10.5334/joc.501). [A] Two preregistered experiments (Exp1 n=501 reduced to 235 after exclusions; Exp2 n=511 reduced to 229). Visual illustrations of how much you already know (0/33/67/100%) shown before each read/skip decision on elephant-conservation chapters. Illustrating a MODERATE gap (33% known) significantly raised the probability of choosing to read; illustrating full knowledge (100%) reduced reading. (Source: WebFetch summary of page; abstract quote: "Illustrating moderate knowledge gaps significantly increased participants' probability of seeking information by reading the chapters.") NOTE: the illustration was a graphic of the gap itself, not a thumbnail; but supports a picture that VISUALISES what is known vs missing.

### 1.2 Headlines: curiosity-gap and question-format evidence
- Le Quere & Matias (2025). When curiosity gaps backfire: effects of headline concreteness on information selection decisions. Scientific Reports 15:994. DOI 10.1038/s41598-024-81575-9. [A - registered report; large field dataset]
  Data: Upworthy Research Archive (A/B tests Jan 2013-Apr 2015; >27,000 tests; the tested unit was a "package" = headline + image; pilot avg CTR 1.33%, median 1.04%). Meta-analysis of 8,977 headline experiments (after filtering).
  Finding (abstract, verified): effect of headline concreteness on CTR depends on how concrete the OTHER headlines in the test are. "When the baseline headline is too vague, higher headline concreteness increases clickthrough rates. When headlines are too concrete, higher headline concreteness decreases clickthrough rates." -> curvilinear: "headlines that convey just the right amount of information maximize clickthrough rates at scale."
  Also verified (Table 1 of paper) - prior experiments on question/curiosity headlines are MIXED: "Across nine reported lab and field experiments, three had a negative, statistically-significant effect, two had a positive, statistically-significant effect, and four were not able to observe outcomes different from random chance". Rows: Scacco & Muddiman 2019 lab question effect -0.03 p<.05 (N=2057); Janet et al 2020 lab question -0.03 n.s. (N=900); Molina et al 2021 question negative p=.003 (N=150); Lai & Farbrot 2014 field question: positive p<.001.
- Fang & Wheeler (2026). Titles framed as questions reduce reader engagement. Journal of Consumer Psychology 36, 470-481 (published 9 Jul 2026). DOI 10.1002/jcpy.70031. [A] Four studies: archival Reddit posts (N=53,030) and academic articles (N=3,078,791), online news-headline A/B experiments (N=22,743), pre-registered lab study (N=400). Verified abstract: "question-framed titles reduce reader engagement because they are viewed as less informative"; conclusion "those wishing to maximize engagement with written content should avoid using questions in their titles." **DIRECT TENSION with the channel rule that every title is a question.** Caveats: written content (articles/posts), not YouTube videos; mechanism = perceived informativeness -> so a title question that is CONCRETE (numbers, named object: "1,900 people... dam that never broke") and a thumbnail that supplies the specific stakes may avoid the penalty. UNVERIFIED for YouTube. Full text was paywalled (403); only abstract read.
- Lai & Farbrot (2014). What makes you click? The effect of question headlines on readership in computer-mediated communication. Social Influence 9(4): 289-299. DOI 10.1080/15534510.2013.847859. [A] Two field experiments (Twitter; FINN.no marketplace). A web-search summary reported question headlines +150% clicks (without self-referencing cues) and +175% (with self-referencing cues) - UNVERIFIED (abstract 403/paywalled; number came from an LLM search summary; Le Quere table lists sample size as "unreported").
- Scacco & Muddiman (2020). The curiosity effect: information seeking in the contemporary news environment. New Media & Society 22(3): 429-448. DOI 10.1177/1461444819863408. [A] Lab + field. Found summary headlines raised perceived information adequacy and expected engagement relative to some curiosity headlines.
- Kuiken, Schuth, Spitters & Marx (2017). Effective headlines of newspaper articles in a digital environment. Digital Journalism 5(10). DOI 10.1080/21670811.2017.1279978. [A] Blendle triples (original vs rewritten headline vs CTR): most clickbait features significantly raised CTR, but CTR could not be predicted from clickbait features alone.
- Cui, Chung, Peng, Wang (2024). Clicks for money: Predicting video views through a sentiment analysis of titles and thumbnails. Journal of Business Research 182?: 114849. DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2024.114849. [A but only secondhand summary]. Web-search summary: strong sentiment in THUMBNAILS (positive or negative) and positive titles lead to more clicks; question-based clickbait titles and strong sentiments in captions generate fewer views. UNVERIFIED wording (ScienceDirect 403); dataset size not retrieved.

### 1.3 Thumbnails/pictures and gap
- ETRA 2018: Yangandul, Paryani, Le & Jain, "How many words is a picture worth? Attention allocation on thumbnails versus title text regions" (DOI 10.1145/3204493.3204571). [A - conference paper, sample size not retrieved] In a casual browsing task (choose a video to watch) participants allocated almost twice as much attention to thumbnails as to title-text regions, and tended to look at thumbnails before the title (project page: jainlab.cise.ufl.edu). Number of participants and platform UNVERIFIED.

## 2. VISUAL ATTENTION / SALIENCY / FEED
- Wolfe & Horowitz (2017). Five factors that guide attention in visual search. Nat Hum Behav 1:0058. DOI 10.1038/s41562-017-0058. [A] Verified abstract: attention is guided by five factors: bottom-up salience, top-down feature guidance, scene structure and meaning, previous search history (msec to years), relative value of targets/distractors. Wolfe & Horowitz (2004) Nat Rev Neurosci 5:495-501 DOI 10.1038/nrn1411 [A] (existence verified; abstract not retrieved; "undoubted" guiding features = colour, motion, orientation, size - FROM MEMORY, UNVERIFIED wording).
- Henderson & Hayes (2017). Meaning maps. Nat Hum Behav 1:743-747. DOI 10.1038/s41562-017-0208-0. [A] Verified: when meaning and salience are correlated-controlled, only MEANING explains unique variance in where people look, from the earliest time-point. Einhauser, Spain & Perona (2008) J Vis 8(14):18: objects (recalled by observers) predict fixations better than early saliency (colour/contrast/etc.), irrespective of task. [A] => "be bright/contrasty" alone is not enough; the thing that matters is a recognisable, meaningful object/event.
- Rosenholtz, Li & Nakano (2007). Measuring visual clutter. J Vis 7(2):17. DOI 10.1167/7.2.17. [A] Verified: excess/disorganised items cause crowding, masking, worse segmentation and search; feature congestion (incl. COLOUR VARIABILITY) matters for clutter. Model: the more cluttered, the harder to add an item that reliably draws attention.
- Pieters & Wedel (2004). Attention capture and transfer in advertising. J Marketing 68(2):36-50. DOI 10.1509/jmkg.68.2.36.27794. [A] Verified abstract (PDF): 1,363 print ads, >3,600 consumers, infrared eye tracking. "The pictorial is superior in capturing attention, independent of its size. The text element best captures attention in direct proportion to its surface size. The brand element most effectively transfers attention to the other elements. Only increments in the text element's surface size produce a net gain in attention to the advertisement as a whole." (magazines, not feeds/phones)
- Pieters, Wedel & Batra (2010). The stopping power of advertising. J Marketing 74(5):48-60. DOI 10.1509/jmkg.74.5.048. [A] Verified abstract: 249 ads with eye tracking; FEATURE complexity (dense perceptual features; JPEG file size proxy) hurts attention to the brand and attitude to the ad; DESIGN complexity (elaborate creative design) HELPS attention to pictorial and to the whole ad and attitude. => "simple" is not the same as "sparse": avoid dense texture/noise, but a designed, meaningful composition can help.
- Rayner, Rotello, Stewart, Keir & Duffy (2001). J Exp Psychol Appl 7(3):219-226. DOI 10.1037/1076-898X.7.3.219. [A] Verified: in print ads viewers spent more time on text than on picture, read the LARGE print first, then smaller print, then the picture (some did a cursory picture scan first). CONTRADICTS the "picture first" framing of ETRA 2018 (thumbnails vs titles) - different stimuli (ads with paragraphs of text vs video grid); large text is read early when present.
- Yangandul et al. ETRA 2018 (see 1.3): thumbnails got ~2x the attention of title text, looked at first. [A-, details UNVERIFIED]
- Tatler (2007) J Vis 7(14):4. DOI 10.1167/7.14.4 [A] and Bindemann (2010) Vision Res 50:2577-2587. DOI 10.1016/j.visres.2010.08.016 [A]: verified - strong central fixation bias at scene onset, independent of image feature distribution and task; Bindemann: also a screen-centre bias that "forms a potential artifact" in lab experiments. => Central bias is real for lab screens; on a scrolling phone feed it is UNVERIFIED (the F-pattern claim comes from Nielsen Norman text-page eye-tracking; NOT tested here, treat as folklore for thumbnails).
- Palmer, Gardner & Wickens (2008). Spatial Vision 21(3-5):421-449. DOI 10.1163/156856808784532662 [A] Verified abstract: for front-facing single objects/people, aesthetic preference greatest at or near the CENTER of the frame ("center bias"); left/right-facing subjects preferred to face INTO the frame ("inward bias"). => Rule-of-thirds is not supported for single-subject pictures (preference study, not CTR). 
- Anderson, Laurent & Yantis (2011). Value-driven attentional capture. PNAS 108(25):10367. DOI 10.1073/pnas.1104047108 [A] Verified: an inconspicuous task-irrelevant item previously associated with reward slows search and captures attention "independently of goals and salience", persistently. Inference (mine, flagged): a channel look associated with videos that paid off can capture attention in a feed.
- Whitney & Levi (2011). Visual crowding. TICS 15:160-168. DOI 10.1016/j.tics.2011.02.005 [A] (existence only; abstract not retrieved) - crowding limits recognition of clustered items in periphery.
- Legge & Bigelow (2011). Does print size matter for reading? J Vis 11(5):8. DOI 10.1167/11.5.8 [A] Verified: fluent reading range spans x-heights 0.2 deg-2 deg (at 40 cm: 1.4 mm-14 mm). Own back-of-envelope (assumptions: phone held ~35-40 cm; full-width feed thumbnail ~6.5 cm wide x 3.7 cm high): a title word 25% of thumb height has cap-height ~0.9 cm, x-height ~0.5 cm = ~0.7 deg = comfortably fluent; the same layout in a ~2 cm-wide list thumbnail is ~3x smaller = x-height ~0.15 cm = ~0.2 deg, at the edge of fluent range. UNVERIFIED as measurement; no study found that gives an evidence-based word-count limit for thumbnails ("max 3 words" advice = creator convention [C]).
- Potter et al. (2014) Atten Percept Psychophys 76:270-279 (meaning detected at 13 ms/picture) [A] vs Maguire & Howe (2016) 78:1405-1413 "Failure to detect meaning in RSVP at 27 ms per picture" [A] - contested; safe claim = gist of a scene/face is extracted within roughly a glance (~100 ms class; Crouzet et al. saccades to faces earliest 100-110 ms, mean ~140 ms), not 13 ms.
- Lindgaard, Fernandes, Dudek & Brown (2006). Behaviour & Information Technology 25:115-126. DOI 10.1080/01449290500330448 (existence verified; "50 ms" visual-appeal judgements; numbers UNVERIFIED here).
- Facebook IQ / Neuro-Insight & Fors Marsh (2016): "people spend 1.7 seconds with a piece of content on mobile; 0.25 s of exposure is enough to recall" - [B] company-commissioned, Facebook not YouTube, primary document not opened; the mi-3.com.au piece (2020) titled "'Fools errand'... is 1.7 seconds enough exposure for ads to work" is critical. Treat as UNVERIFIED-order-of-magnitude ("about 2 seconds").
- Number of elements in ~1 s: fixations ~3-4 per second (standard eye-movement fact; not verified in this session - UNVERIFIED); visual working memory ~3-4 items (Luck & Vogel 1997; Cowan 2001 - from memory, UNVERIFIED here). Design rule derived: 1 dominant subject + 1 secondary object + <=1 short text = ~3 items.

## 3. FACES / SCHEMATIC FACES / EMOTION
- Crouzet, Kirchner & Thorpe (2010). Fast saccades toward faces. J Vis 10(4):16. DOI 10.1167/10.4.16 [A] Verified: earliest reliable saccades toward (photographic) human faces 100-110 ms, mean ~140 ms; still biased to faces when targeting vehicles.
- Hershler & Hochstein (2005) Vision Res 45:1707-1724 [A]: natural (photo) faces pop out among non-face objects; their own abstract states "A number of previous studies suggested that schematic faces do not pop out". VanRullen (2006) Vision Res 46:3017-3027 [A]: the pop-out effect replicates but is mostly explained by low-level Fourier amplitude/contour information; no face-specific asymmetry. => Face capture is real for photos; for schematic faces pre-attentive pop-out is doubtful.
- Ohman, Lundqvist & Esteves (2001). JPSP 80(3):381-396. DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.80.3.381 [A] Verified: SCHEMATIC threatening faces detected faster/more accurately than friendly ones in 5 experiments (also inverted). Becker, Anderson, Mortensen, Neufeld & Neel (2011). J Exp Psychol Gen 140(4):637-659. DOI 10.1037/a0024060 [A] Verified: 7 experiments controlling confounds found NO angry pop-out, instead a happy-face advantage. => CONTRADICTION: "angry/threat faces pop out" is contested; do not rely on it. Schematic faces DO carry emotion.
- Kendall, Raffaelli, Kingstone & Todd (2016). Iconic faces are not real faces. Cogn Res Principles Implic 1:19. DOI 10.1186/s41235-016-0021-8 [A] Verified: 5 stimulus sets from photorealistic to fully iconic; at SHORT presentation times, emotion-identification accuracy was HIGHER for more cartoonised images; higher contrast and lower featural complexity helped; EEG P1 lower for low-contrast/low-complexity schematic stimuli (driven by low-level features). => STRONGEST direct evidence for stick-figure faces: iconic faces communicate emotion quickly, especially with high contrast + low complexity.
- Churches, Nicholls, Thiessen, Kohler & Keage (2014). Emoticons in mind. Soc Neurosci 9(2):196-202. DOI 10.1080/17470919.2013.873737 [A] Verified: upright emoticons ":-)" produce a large N170 (face-sensitive ERP) that DROPS when inverted (opposite of real faces) => processed like faces only in their familiar upright configuration; n=20.
- Pareidolia: Wardle, Taubert, Teichmann & Baker (2020) Nat Commun 11:4518 DOI 10.1038/s41467-020-18325-8 [A]: illusory faces initially represented like real faces (MEG) but within ~250 ms become equivalent to objects ("broadly-tuned face detection... privileges sensitivity over selectivity"). Keys, Taubert & Wardle (2021) Atten Percept Psychophys 83:1942-1953 DOI 10.3758/s13414-021-02267-4 [A]: search faster for illusory faces than matched objects, but real human faces faster still. Liu et al. (2014) Cortex 53:60-77 [A]: face pareidolia recruits right FFA. => faces in objects (a dam/bridge with a "worried" look) is testable but weaker than a real/character face.
- Looser & Wheatley (2010). Psychol Sci 21:1854-1862 [A] Verified: perception of life/mind in faces has a categorical tipping point near the human end; impression of life gleaned from the EYES more than other features.
- Farroni et al. (2005) PNAS 102:17245-17250 [A]: newborns prefer schematic upright face-relevant patterns only if darker areas around eyes and mouth (contrast polarity). Supports high-contrast dark eye/mouth marks on a light head (stick-figure design already does this).
- Whalen et al. (2004). Science 306:2061. DOI 10.1126/science.1103617 [A] Verified: amygdala more responsive to masked fearful (larger) eye whites than happy (smaller) eye whites. Caveat: eye WHITES (sclera) - a dot-eye stick figure has none; wide-open eyes = enlarge the dot/ring? UNTESTED for cartoons.
- Senju & Johnson (2009). The eye contact effect. TICS 13:127-134 [A] Verified: perceived eye contact modulates concurrent processing; preferential orienting toward direct-gaze faces from early in life.
- Sajjacholapunt & Ball (2014). Front Psychol 5:166. DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00166 [A] Verified abstract via Frontiers: N=72 (ages 18-32), eye tracking on webpages with banner ads: no face vs face with MUTUAL gaze vs face with AVERTED gaze. Averted gaze increased attention to banner, ad text and product and improved memory (brand recognition items: averted 1.50 > mutual 1.06 > no face 0.54; word-fragment completion 64.48% for averted vs ~42-48% otherwise); mutual gaze kept attention on the face and weakened product attention. => a figure looking AT the hazard beats a figure staring at the viewer if the goal is to point at the story object; mutual gaze wins face attention only.
- Friesen & Kingstone (1998). Psychonomic Bull Rev 5:490-495 (DOI 10.3758/BF03208827) [A] - per web summary: the cue was a SCHEMATIC face (outline circle, two eye circles with pupils, line mouth); pupil direction produced faster target detection at the gazed side. Ristic, Friesen & Kingstone (2002) PB&R 9:507-513 [A] verified: arrows produce the same reflexive orienting => any directional cue works (a pointing arm, a leaning pole) but ARROWS ARE FORBIDDEN by the brand rule inside images; use gaze/pose/a bent object instead. Frischen, Bayliss & Tipper (2007) Psychol Bull 133:694-724 [A review].
- Negativity of face: Vuilleumier (2005) TICS 9:585-594 [A] verified: amygdala provides top-down signals boosting representation of emotional (esp. threat) events. Yiend (2010) Cogn Emot 24:3-47 [A review, abstract not retrieved].
- NOT SEARCHED / UNVERIFIED: emotional contagion from cartoon or emoji faces (facial mimicry of emoticons); exaggeration (caricature) effect on attention; faces-without-eyes. Say so.

## 4. THREAT, DANGER, ANOMALY, SCALE, SAFE DANGER
- Robertson, Prollochs, Schwarzenegger, Parnamets, Van Bavel (2023). Negativity drives online news consumption. Nat Hum Behav 7:812-822. DOI 10.1038/s41562-023-01538-4 [A] Verified: RCTs N=22,743 (Upworthy); ~105,000 headline variations; ~5.7 million clicks; >370 million impressions; each additional NEGATIVE word in a headline of average length raised CTR by 2.3% (positive words lowered it). NB same archive as Le Quere & Matias; headlines not thumbnails.
- Soroka, Fournier & Nir (2019). PNAS 116(38):18888-18892 [A] Verified: 17-country, 6-continent psychophysiology study of real video news: negativity bias on average, with considerable individual variation.
- New, Cosmides & Tooby (2007). PNAS 104:16598-16603 [A] verified: change detection faster for animals than for any inanimate category incl. vehicles. LoBue & DeLoache (2008). Psychol Sci 19:284-289 [A] verified: adults and preschoolers detect snakes faster than flowers/frogs/caterpillars. => attention to ANIMATE/evolutionary threats is well supported; attention to engineered hazards (cracks, dams, bridges) is NOT directly shown (UNVERIFIED) - the appeal must come from meaning/curiosity, not from innate threat detection.
- Anomaly / incongruity: Loftus & Mackworth (1978) J Exp Psychol HPP 4:565-572 (existence verified; classic finding from memory: semantically odd objects fixated earlier) vs Vo & Henderson (2009) J Vis 9(3):24 [A] verified: NO evidence of extrafoveal detection of semantic or syntactic (floating) inconsistencies - they attract gaze only after fixation, with longer gaze durations; Vo & Henderson (2011) APP 73:1742-1753 "Object-scene inconsistencies do not capture gaze" [A] (title verified). => MYTH-risk: "something wrong in the picture grabs the eye at thumbnail glance" is NOT supported; an anomaly rewards a second look AFTER the eye lands there. Put the anomaly where the eye already goes (the subject/centre).
- Meyers-Levy & Tybout (1989). Schema congruity. J Consumer Res 16:39-54 DOI 10.1086/209192 (existence verified; finding - moderate incongruity best - from memory, UNVERIFIED).
- Mobbs et al. (2007). When fear is near. Science 317:1079-1083. DOI 10.1126/science.1144298 [A] Verified: as a virtual predator got closer, brain activity shifted vmPFC -> periaqueductal gray; PAG activity correlated with dread. Threat IMMINENCE changes the state. Only supports "closer = more dread" in a maze task, NOT the thumbnail claim "moment before beats aftermath" - no study found comparing before/after-disaster thumbnails; UNVERIFIED. (Rationale is Loewenstein: before = open gap; aftermath = gap already closed.)
- Proulx (2010). Size matters. PLoS ONE 5(12):e15293 [A] verified: an irrelevantly LARGE object captured attention in visual search (stimulus-driven). Supports big-structure scale contrast for capture; no study of tiny-figure-vs-huge-structure "awe" in thumbnails.
- Awe: Keltner & Haidt (2003) Cogn Emot 17:297-314 [A] (existence verified; vastness + need for accommodation - from memory). Piff et al. (2015) JPSP 108:883-899 "Awe, the small self, and prosocial behavior" (NOT retrieved - the PubMed hit was a different 2024 paper; UNVERIFIED). Liao et al. (2024) Front Psychol 15:1336068 [A] awe -> small-self -> forgiveness (mediation), tangential.
- Safe danger: Rozin, Guillot, Fincher, Rozin & Tsukayama (2013). Glad to be sad, and other examples of benign masochism. Judgment and Decision Making 8:439-447. DOI 10.1017/S1930297500005295 [A] Verified: 29 initially aversive activities give pleasure via "hedonic reversals" (incl. fear); explanation = enjoyment of negative bodily reactions "in the context of feeling safe"; "the favored level of initially negative experiences is just below the level that cannot be tolerated". Morbid curiosity (Scrivner 2021, Zuckerman & Litle 1986) NOT verified this session -> UNVERIFIED.
- Ethics: Lee & Feeley (2016). The identifiable victim effect: a meta-analytic review. Social Influence 11:199-215. DOI 10.1080/15534510.2016.1216891 [A] existence verified; effect size (reported r about .05) FROM MEMORY - UNVERIFIED; if right, the "one named/individual victim beats statistics" creator advice is weak. No ethics-of-thumbnail research found; ethical framing here = brief rules (failure is the villain; no gore) + platform policy (not researched).

## 5. FLUENCY / SIMPLICITY / COLOUR
- Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman (2004). PSPR 8:364-382. DOI 10.1207/s15327957pspr0804_3 [A] Verified: aesthetic pleasure = f(processing fluency); reviewed variables: figural goodness, figure-ground contrast, stimulus repetition, symmetry, prototypicality; visual/semantic priming also raises judged pleasure.
- Alter & Oppenheimer (2009). PSPR 13:219-235. DOI 10.1177/1088868309341564 [A] Verified: fluency (perceptual, conceptual, linguistic) reliably shifts judgments, e.g. judged more TRUE when visually clear or semantically primed.
- Le Quere & Matias (2025) + Kidd (2012): "simple" is not automatically best - engagement peaks at INTERMEDIATE complexity/information (Goldilocks). Design: simple to PARSE, not empty of meaning.
- Colour: Valdez & Mehrabian (1994). J Exp Psychol Gen 123:394-409. DOI 10.1037/0096-3445.123.4.394 [A] Verified (Munsell chips, PAD model): SATURATION and BRIGHTNESS had strong consistent effects on emotion: Pleasure=.69B+.22S; Arousal=-.31B+.60S; blue/green/purples most pleasant, yellow & green-yellow least pleasant; most arousing = green-yellow, blue-green, green (NOT red). => arousal tracks saturation more than a specific hue; "red = excitement" is not what the data show for chips.
  Elliot & Maier (2014). Annu Rev Psychol 65:95-120. DOI 10.1146/annurev-psych-010213-115035 [A]: colour effects are context-dependent (colour-in-context theory), methodological issues, generalisability needed "before strong conceptual statements and recommendations for application are warranted" (via web summary; full text not opened).
  Palmer & Schloss ecological valence theory (2010 PNAS) - a 2026 test (Epicoco, Mohr & Jonauskaite, Q J Exp Psychol 79:2130) supports the central prediction (people like blue, dislike brownish yellows; N=135) [A].
  MYTH: "colour increases brand recognition by up to 80%" - widely attributed to a Loyola (Maryland) study by Dr Ellen Hoadley; sources (insights4print.ceo 2019/2025 [C]) say it was about colour vs monochrome in documents/newspaper ads, not brand-vs-brand palettes; treat as UNSUPPORTED for thumbnails.
  Pop-out is RELATIVE contrast (Wolfe & Horowitz; Rosenholtz clutter): no hue is universally "attention-grabbing"; it must differ from its surroundings. In the YouTube feed the surroundings are white/black UI plus busy photographic thumbnails, so flat-colour cartoons already stand apart; avoiding YouTube-red UI colours for the key element is a plausible but UNTESTED creator heuristic [C].
- Von Restorff/isolation: Hunt (1995) Psychonomic Bull Rev 2:105-112 [A] (existence verified; "distinctiveness is relative to the context") - one element different from the rest is remembered/attended (isolation effect).
- Symmetry/central composition: Reber 2004 lists symmetry as fluency-raising; Palmer 2008 centre bias. No CTR test.

## 6. FAMILIARITY / BRANDING / HABITUATION
- Montoya, Horton, Vevea, Citkowicz & Lauber (2017). Psychol Bull 143(5):459-498. DOI 10.1037/bul0000085 [A] Verified: meta-analysis, 268 curve estimates from 81 articles: mere-exposure effect is an INVERTED-U in exposure (positive slope, negative quadratic); inverted-U curves for all visual (not auditory) stimuli, for exposure durations <10 s and >1 min, both homogeneous and heterogeneous presentation. => liking rises with repeats then declines; short glances (thumbnails) are in the range where the effect is found.

## CONTRADICTIONS / MYTHS SUMMARY
1. "Question titles boost clicks": mixed. Lai & Farbrot 2014 positive; Scacco & Muddiman 2020, Molina 2021 negative; Le Quere table: 3 negative, 2 positive, 4 null of 9 experiments; Fang & Wheeler 2026 (N=22,743 A/B tests etc.) question titles REDUCE engagement (perceived as less informative). => flag against the brief's title rule; mitigation = concreteness.
2. "Curiosity gap = hide info": Le Quere & Matias: too vague AND too concrete both lose; optimum in between; Frede 2026: moderate gap illustrations raise reading, full-knowledge illustrations lower it.
3. "Anomaly/angry face pops out": Becker 2011 vs Ohman 2001; Vo & Henderson 2009/2011.
4. "Colour X = clicks": no. Saturation/brightness > hue (Valdez & Mehrabian); pop-out is relative contrast.
5. "Rule of thirds": Palmer 2008 centre preference for single subjects.
6. "F-pattern / faces always first on thumbnails": F-pattern not verified for thumbnails; faces capture is strongest for photographic faces (Crouzet, Hershler); schematic-face pop-out doubtful (Hershler intro; VanRullen). Picture-first (ETRA 2018) vs text-first (Rayner 2001 print ads).
7. "80% brand-recognition from colour": unsupported.
8. "Negative headlines win": supported for words in headlines (Robertson 2023, +2.3% CTR per negative word) - but headline data, not images.
